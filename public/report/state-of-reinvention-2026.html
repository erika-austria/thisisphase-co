A Letter from Erika

No one hands you a map for all of it at once.

When I published the first State of the Union two years ago, I wrote it as a CEO documenting the resilience of mothers in business. Everything I said then was true. It was also only one room of the house.

Since then my own life did what the data in these pages describes. My body started changing in my mid-thirties, a decade before any book said it should. My marriage ended at thirty-eight. I was rebuilding my voice and my company at the same time I was raising four boys and trying to understand a body that no longer felt like mine. I did not go through those things in a tidy sequence. I went through them in the same season, all at once, with no one handing me a plan.

That is the difference in this report. Reinvention is not a single event you survive and move past. It is four rooms a woman is often living in at the same time: the Body that changes without permission, the Family she rebuilds after it breaks, the Voice she stops apologizing for, and the Work she builds in the margins the world left her. This is the State of Reinvention, and the numbers are staggering, hopeful, and long overdue.

I built HeyMama and led it through an acquisition. I co-founded NEON ID to study how we show up. And I built The PHASE™ because I needed the tools in my own hands before anyone would put them there. What I know now is that the woman coming apart in her forties is not falling behind. She is the first person in her life brave enough to stop pretending.

The stats that follow prove the cost. They also prove the agency. Read them as evidence of what you already feel: you are not the exception. You are the majority, and the future being built is yours.

MOMumentally,

The Thesis

You are not in a phase.
You are in The PHASE™.

Why a State of Reinvention, and why now.

For most of a woman's life she is told that the hard seasons are detours. That the exhaustion is temporary, the body is misbehaving, the marriage is her failure to manage, the self-doubt is hers to fix quietly. She is told to get back to normal.

The data says otherwise. What we have been calling personal crisis is, at scale, the ordinary architecture of the modern female midlife. More than half of women aged 30 to 35 already report moderate to severe perimenopause symptoms. Women file the majority of divorces and pay the steepest price for choosing themselves. They are interrupted at the Supreme Court and dismissed in the exam room. And they are opening businesses faster than any other group in America while capturing barely two percent of the capital.

Put those truths in one place and a pattern appears. Reinvention is not the breakdown. It is the redesign. It happens in four rooms, and a woman is usually standing in more than one of them at the same time.

I
Body

II
Family

III
Voice
The sentence she finally says out loud.

Work

This report walks through all four, with the numbers, the cost, and the reason each one is a doorway and not a dead end.

Room One

TheBody

The body keeps the score before the calendar does. Reinvention rarely begins as a choice. It begins as a symptom no one will name.
Perimenopause · Hormones · Midlife Health
Room One · The Body

The change arrives a decade early, and almost no one is ready.

The average American woman reaches menopause at 51. The symptomatic runway begins as much as a decade earlier, which means many women are inside the transition in their late thirties and early forties and never name it. A 2025 study found that more than half of women aged 30 to 35, and nearly two thirds aged 36 to 40, already report moderate to severe perimenopause symptoms. About 1.3 million US women reach menopause every year, roughly six thousand a day.

The symptoms are rarely dramatic. That is what makes them dangerous. It is the volume turned down on your own life, one notch at a time, easy to blame on cortisol and a full calendar.

The care gap is real, and it is not in your head.

Here is what turns a normal transition into a silent emergency. Nearly nine in ten women with symptoms never seek care. Only about 31 percent of OB-GYN residency programs teach a standardized menopause curriculum, even though 93 percent of residents say one should exist. And 94 percent of women report no menopause education in school. Most women are left to self-diagnose in real time, then are dismissed when they ask.

Room Two

TheFamily

This is where the myth of the broken home goes to die. The data proves the cost. It also proves the agency.
Divorce · Co-Parenting · Blended & Solo Motherhood
Room Two · The Family

Look at who is choosing it.

Every headline frames divorce as something that happens to a woman. The data says she is usually the one deciding. Women file roughly 69 percent of divorces, and the median woman now divorces at almost 41, squarely in midlife. The fastest growing group of divorced adults is people over 50: gray divorce among those 65 and older has tripled since 1990. These are not women falling apart. They are women who spent decades carrying the household and decided the arrangement no longer worked.

45%
drop in a woman's standard of living after gray divorce, versus 21% for men.
Lin & Brown, 2020
71%
of the household mental load is carried by mothers, the invisible work of planning and remembering.
Journal of Marriage and Family, 2024
Gray divorce is not a family falling apart. It is, disproportionately, a woman deciding the arrangement no longer works and building a truer one, even knowing the financial cliff is real.

The cost is real. So is the rebuild.

The financial picture is honest and hard. Single-mother families face a poverty rate of 31.3 percent, nearly six times the rate for married couples, and roughly 80 percent of custodial parents are mothers. Only 20 percent of women repartner after a gray divorce, compared with 31 percent of men. Women more often rebuild the household, and rebuild it alone. The reinvention is not pretending the cost away. It is making the invisible labor visible, valued, and finally hers to allocate.

Room Three

TheVoice

The most expensive thing a woman owns is the sentence she did not say. In this room, reinvention stops being a metaphor and becomes measurable.
Confidence · Self-Trust · Being Believed
Room Three · The Voice

The swallowed sentence has a body count.

For decades women were rewarded for editing themselves. Likeable, agreeable, not too much. The research now shows what that costs. Women who consistently silenced themselves during marital conflict were four times more likely to die over a ten year follow-up than women who spoke up, even after adjusting for the usual risk factors. In midlife women, higher self-silencing tracks with measurable plaque buildup in the arteries. Voice is not a soft skill. It is tied to survival.

39.4%

JAMA Network Open, 2025
10 to 11 yrs
average wait for an endometriosis or adenomyosis diagnosis.
Journal of Women's Health, 2026
The un-pushed-back-on symptom becomes a ten-year diagnostic delay. The un-applied-for role becomes a career that stalled at 100 percent qualified. Reinvention is finally using the voice that was always there.

The double bind, and the turn.

Women are interrupted 33 percent more often than men, and female Supreme Court justices are cut off roughly three times as often as their male colleagues. Robes and lifetime appointments do not close the gap. Three in four women executives report imposter feelings. And women still self-authorize less: the widely cited finding is that women apply for a role when they meet 100 percent of the qualifications, while men apply at about 60. Then comes the turn. In a 2026 survey, 77 percent of women over 50 said they had reached a point where they no longer worry what others think, and 75 percent now trust their instincts over seeking approval.

Who is changing this room

Katty Kay and Claire Shipman, authors of The Confidence Code, made the confidence gap mainstream and argued it is buildable. Mary Ann Sieghart, author of The Authority Gap, documented with data how women are still taken less seriously. Dr. Mary Claire Haver became the face of “you are not crazy, you are not alone” in women's health.

TheWork

Women are the fastest growing force in American business, and they are doing it against the current.
Mompreneurs · Economics · Building Through It
Room Four · The Work

The fastest growth in the economy, built in the margins.

Women now own 14.5 million US businesses, nearly 4 in 10 of all firms, generating 3.3 trillion dollars in revenue, up from 2.7 trillion just a couple of years ago. From 2019 to 2024 women-owned firms grew faster than men-owned firms on every metric that matters: 17 percent more firms, nearly 20 percent more jobs, and almost 54 percent more revenue. Mothers are the fastest growing engine inside that number. This is not leaning in. It is rebuilding a machine that was never designed for a mother to run.

$10.2T
the revenue women-owned firms would add if they matched men-owned averages. Parity is 120 years away at the current pace.
Wells Fargo, 2025
61.8¢
what mothers earn per father's dollar. Black mothers 48.8¢, Latina mothers 42.7¢.
IWPR, 2025
Women own 39 percent of the businesses and receive 2 percent of the funding. That math is not a footnote. That math is the reinvention.

The cost shows up in the body, again.

The pressure is not free. In 2025, 68 percent of women reported feeling burned out, compared with 42 percent of men. The share of mothers rating their own mental health as excellent fell from 38 percent in 2016 to 26 percent in 2023. The Work room and the Body room are the same story told twice: a woman carrying a system that was not built to hold her, and building anyway.

Who is changing this room

Emma Grede, co-founder of Good American and founding partner of SKIMS, built a multibillion dollar portfolio as a mother of four. Sara Blakely bootstrapped Spanx to a billion. Whitney Wolfe Herd took Bumble public and returned as CEO. Jessica Alba built The Honest Company from her own values as a new mom.
